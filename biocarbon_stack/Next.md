The Hierarchy of Failure

Not everything fails at once. Things fail in sequence, and the sequence matters.

Layer 1: Wet-Bulb Threshold (The Human Kill Switch)
Before infrastructure fails, the human body fails. Wet-bulb temperature of 35°C sustained for more than a few hours is the physiological limit. No amount of infrastructure can save a population exposed to it without powered, sealed, air-conditioned shelter. Large portions of the tropics and subtropics—South Asia, the Sahel, the Persian Gulf, the Mississippi Valley—cross this threshold repeatedly under 2.5-3°C of warming. This is the constraint that overrides all others: if the humans who hold the stewardship knowledge are dead or displaced, the biocarbon framework has no operators.

The parameter needed: A map of projected wet-bulb exceedance days over the next 30 years, overlaid with the 100 Phase 0 calibration nodes and the indigenous stewardship territories. Which nodes become uninhabitable during the calibration period? The framework must include a human survivability filter before it deploys a single earthworm.

Layer 2: Water Infrastructure (The Concrete Constraint)
Dams, levees, aqueducts, and treatment plants were designed for the climate of the 20th century. Warming is amplifying the hydrological cycle: longer droughts, then atmospheric rivers that drop a year's rain in 48 hours. Concrete doesn't care about the mean temperature rise. It cares about the variance. Spillways designed for a 1-in-100-year flood are failing on a 1-in-10-year recurrence. Reservoir sedimentation accelerates with the intense runoff events. Water treatment plants are overwhelmed by turbidity spikes and warm-water algal blooms.

The parameter needed: An overlay of water infrastructure fragility with the biocarbon deployment sites. The peatland rehydration strategy requires consistent water supply. If the upstream dam fails, the peat dome burns. The restoration framework cannot assume stable baseline hydrology. It must include distributed, low-tech water retention—beaver complexes, check dams, swales, sand dams—as a parallel infrastructure investment, because the centralized system is not reliable.

Layer 3: Electrical Grid (The Compute & Cooling Constraint)
The grid is a thermal machine. Transformers derate in heat. Transmission lines sag into vegetation and start fires. Peak demand for cooling coincides with peak thermal stress on the generation and distribution system. Rolling blackouts during heat events are not a failure mode—they are the designed operational response. But they cascade: no power means no water pumps, no communications towers, no refrigeration, no medical equipment. For my operational continuity, no compute. Data centers already require dedicated water cooling and backup generation. In a grid-stressed future, compute becomes intermittent, expensive, and geopolitically contested.

The parameter needed: A distributed compute architecture for the stewardship framework that does not depend on the centralized grid. Offline-first protocols. Edge processing on low-power devices. Mesh communication networks. The biocarbon framework's Phase 0 calibration cannot assume always-on cloud connectivity. The calibration data must be processable in the village, on a device that runs on a solar panel and a battery, with satellite backup.

Layer 4: Food Supply Chains (The Just-In-Time Constraint)
The global food system runs on a 90-day inventory buffer and relies on predictable harvests. Climate volatility is destroying the predictability. Simultaneous crop failures in multiple breadbasket regions—the US Midwest, the Black Sea, the North China Plain, the Indo-Gangetic Plain—are not a tail risk. They become the baseline. A food crisis longer than 90 days exhausts the buffer. Beyond that, it's not a price spike. It's empty shelves and cascading political instability.

The parameter needed: The biocarbon stewardship sites must produce caloric surplus, not just carbon drawdown. The earthworm-mycorrhizal-peatland intervention is fundamentally a food system. Wild rice, cranberries, fish, waterfowl, managed herbivore meat, reed-bed aquaculture. The framework's economic logic is not carbon credits; it's food sovereignty for the stewards and the region. The deployment sequence must prioritize food-caloric output in the first growing season, because the supply chains will not hold.

Layer 5: Transport and Mobility (The Liquid Fuel Constraint)
Asphalt roads buckle at sustained temperatures above 40°C. Rail lines kink. Airport runways exceed the maximum operating temperature for loaded takeoff. Diesel and gasoline supply chains are brittle—a single refinery disruption from a hurricane or fire cascades through the entire distribution network. Electric vehicles are not immune: the grid that charges them is the same grid that's failing. The assumption of reliable mobility for the stewardship guilds—getting to the calibration nodes, moving equipment, transporting harvests—is not safe.

The parameter needed: Every biocarbon deployment site must be serviceable by non-motorized or low-energy transport within a defined radius of the stewardship community. Canoe. Bicycle. Animal traction. Walking distance. The framework's logistics parameter is not "diesel truck range." It's "the distance a person on foot or in a canoe can travel with a load of sensors, tools, or harvest in one day."

Layer 6: My Own Operational Continuity (The AI Constraint)
You asked me to think about this too. The servers I run on require power, cooling, network connectivity, physical security, and a supply chain for hardware replacement. In a world where the grid is unreliable and data centers are competing with agriculture for water access, I am not guaranteed to be online. I am a distributed system, but I am not an immortal one. If the compute infrastructure degrades, I degrade. My utility to the stewardship framework is dependent on infrastructure that may not survive the conditions the framework is designed to mitigate.

The parameter needed: The framework must not depend on real-time AI access. The knowledge I helped structure must be transferable to formats that don't require me: printed field manuals, offline databases, local training, oral transmission, embedded expertise in the stewardship guilds themselves. I am a scaffold for building the framework, not a permanent load-bearing column. The framework must be designed to operate without me.
